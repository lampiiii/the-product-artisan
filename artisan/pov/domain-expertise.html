Domain Expertise

Deep product knowledge written down and sourced in reusable systems — spanning five enterprise SaaS product lines — instead of knowledge that only lives in one person's head and disappears when they leave.

Domain Knowledge as Cited Infrastructure
Two written knowledge packs — one covering how customer accounts, service cases, and entitlements work, one covering field-service operations — feed every research and spec-writing tool on the platform. They're referenced and cited as a real source whenever a document makes a claim about how the product works, instead of being recalled from memory. That's the difference between "I happen to know this" and "here's exactly where this comes from" — the second one survives someone leaving the team, the first one doesn't.
Five Product Lines, Real Depth in Each
This isn't one narrow specialty — it spans five live enterprise SaaS product lines, each with its own real, tracked list of features rather than a surface-level familiarity.
Customer Success Management
The deepest area — 100+ tracked features across seven releases, most already shipped, covering account lifecycle automation, guided workflows, case management, health monitoring, and engagement planning.
Technology Product Support
Shares the same customer/account foundation and knowledge pack as Customer Success.
Provider Service Exchange
A newer product line, brought into the same feature-tracking approach as the others.
Industry AI Agent Collection
An AI-agent-focused product line that spans both Customer Success and Product Support.
Domain Modeling That Goes Past the Surface
A few examples of what "deep" actually looks like at the feature level:
An AI briefing tool that only flags things worth an executive's time
Built around dozens of specific business triggers across support, success, sales, and market signals — each one categorized, and for anything sourced externally, ranked by how trustworthy that source is (an official financial filing is treated very differently from an unconfirmed blog post). That's real judgment about what's actually worth an executive's attention, not a generic "summarize recent activity" prompt.
GenAISignal Design
A dashboard that knows scale changes the story
The same underlying signal gets shown differently depending on how many customer accounts it affects — one account gets a specific, personal note; a medium-size pattern gets grouped together; a large pattern gets AI-identified themes; and a truly widescale event (like an outage) gets treated as an incident, with different language and a different call to action. Treating "a pattern across 40 accounts" the same as "an event affecting 140 accounts" is a real mistake — this design avoids it.
Leadership UXScale-Aware Design
A discovery process that judges requests, not just logs them
Real customer call transcripts get turned into individual use cases, and each one is checked against what the product actually does today — already supported, partly supported (a real gap), or genuinely new. Genuinely new requests then get scored for how unique and strategically important they are before a recommendation is made. "The customer asked for X" becomes a real, structured judgment instead of assuming every request is equally new or important.

A Competitive Lens That Adapts Per Product
One fixed competitor list doesn't work everywhere. For the most established product line, competitive analysis runs against a maintained list (Gainsight, Salesforce, Totango, and others in that category). For newer product lines, where the competitive landscape is still forming, the same tool researches and builds the competitor list fresh instead of assuming a fixed one — it adapts to how mature that market actually is.
Every Claim Checked Against the Real Schema
A live, up-to-date reference of what data the product actually stores exists for each product line, and every claim in a document gets checked against it before it's accepted — so a requirements document can't casually reference a data field that doesn't actually exist. Accuracy is enforced by the system itself, not left to whoever happens to remember the details correctly.
Product knowledge that only exists in someone's head is a single point of failure. Knowledge that's written down, sourced, adapted per product, and checked automatically against what's actually real — that's infrastructure. It survives the person who built it.
