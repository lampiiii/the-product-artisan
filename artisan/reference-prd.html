Reference PRD: Renewal‑Risk Review Agent

A complete product requirements document for one of the 19 agents in the Customer Success agent system — written the way I actually write them, then sanitized for publication. It is here because describing a PRD and showing one are different claims.

Sanitized for publication. Internal system names, product codenames, customer names, and exact internal figures are removed or rounded. The structure, reasoning, and decision boundaries are the real ones. Nothing here is confidential ServiceNow material.

Status
Shipped · GA
Author
Abhay Tyagi, Sr PM
Scope
1 of 19 agents, 5 workflows
Reviewers
Eng, Design, Security, GTM

01Context and problem

Customer Success Managers at enterprise accounts run a renewal-risk review on every account in their book ahead of each renewal cycle. Done manually, the review means pulling usage telemetry, open support cases, health-score movement, executive-sponsor changes, and last-quarter's success-plan progress out of five different places, then forming a judgment about whether the renewal is at risk and what to do about it.

A time study across the CSM organization put this and its adjacent manual work at 200+ hours per week in aggregate. The cost is not only the hours. Because the review is expensive, it happens late — often close enough to the renewal date that the intervention window has already closed. The org was paying for a diagnostic it could not afford to run early enough to act on.

Why this, and why now

Two competitors, Gainsight and Totango, were positioning risk intelligence as a core differentiator in evaluations we were losing. The gap was visible in lost-deal analysis. Meanwhile the agent platform investment was already committed, which meant the marginal cost of adding a well-scoped agent workflow was low relative to building this as bespoke product logic.

02Jobs to be Done

Job statementCurrent workaroundWhat "done well" looks like
When a renewal enters its final two quarters, I want to know which of my accounts are actually at risk, so I can spend my limited hours on the ones where I can change the outcome.Manual review, run late, on a subset of the book chosen by gut feel.Ranked risk list covering the full book, refreshed continuously, with the evidence attached.
When I am told an account is at risk, I want to see why, so I can decide whether I agree before I act on it.Health score with no traceable reasoning; CSMs routinely override it and stop trusting it.Every risk call cites the specific signals behind it and links to the source record.
When I brief my leader on book health, I want to defend the numbers, so I can ask for the resource I need without relitigating the data.Hand-built slides, inconsistent between CSMs.One generated view both the CSM and the leader are looking at.

The second job is the one that decides whether this ships or dies. An agent that produces correct risk calls with no legible reasoning fails in exactly the way the existing health score already failed — it gets overridden, then ignored.

03Prioritization

This competed against manual-check-in tooling and a success-plan template expansion for the same quarter's capacity. Scored with RICE, using CSM time-study data for impact rather than estimates:

The judgment call worth naming: check-in tooling was cheaper, faster, and had louder customer demand. It lost because it optimizes a ritual rather than the decision the ritual exists to serve. Field requests were for a better manual review; the time study said the manual review itself was the problem.

04Goals, non-goals, and success measures

Goals

  • Cut renewal-risk review effort materially, without reducing coverage of the book
  • Surface risk early enough that intervention is still possible
  • Make every risk call auditable back to source records
  • Keep human judgment on every high-risk determination

Non-goals

  • The agent does not contact customers. Ever, in v1 — outbound is a separate trust conversation
  • The agent does not change account or renewal records; it proposes, a human commits
  • No renewal-probability percentage. A false precision number invites arguments about the number instead of the account
  • Not a replacement for the CSM relationship; explicitly a preparation layer

R5 is worth dwelling on. Enforcing access control at the platform layer rather than through agent instructions is the difference between a control and a suggestion. This was the requirement that unblocked deals with banking and finance customers whose security review had been stalling them.

06Decision boundaries

The core design question for any agent is not what it can do — it is what it is permitted to decide alone. This is the table engineering, security, and GTM all argued over, and the one I would bring to any agent review:

DecisionAgent decidesHuman decidesRationale
Which signals are relevant to this account✓Mechanical; high volume; cheap to get wrong and correct
Low / Watch tier assignment✓Reversible, low blast radius, reviewed in aggregate
High tier assignmentProposes✓ ConfirmsTriggers escalation and resourcing; a false positive burns CSM credibility with the account
Insufficient-evidence flag✓Abstaining must always be free — this is what stops confident guessing
Any customer-facing communication✓ AlwaysTrust boundary. Non-negotiable in v1
Record mutation✓ AlwaysAgent proposes, human commits. Keeps the audit trail human-attributable

"Abstaining has to be cheaper for the agent than guessing. If the only way to look useful is to produce a verdict, it will produce a verdict."

07Rollout

Enterprise B2B does not permit clean account-level A/B splits — accounts are not interchangeable and customers notice inconsistent behaviour. So validation was staged by cohort with success criteria fixed in advance:

  • Stage 0 — Shadow. Agent scores the full book; output visible only to the product team. Compared against CSM judgment on the same accounts. Ship gate: agreement rate clears the rubric threshold.
  • Stage 1 — Single-team pilot. One CSM team, agent output visible alongside existing workflow, both running. Ship gate: no regression in review quality, plus qualitative signal that CSMs read the citations rather than the tier alone.
  • Stage 2 — Segment expansion. Widened by segment, not by percentage — regulated-industry accounts last, after security sign-off.
  • Stage 3 — GA, with the manual path still available for a full cycle. Removing the fallback before one complete renewal cycle would have meant discovering seasonal failure modes in production.

08Risks and what we cut

RiskMitigation
Confident wrongness. A plausible, well-written, incorrect risk narrative is more dangerous than an obvious error — it survives review.Citation requirement (R2) plus reviewer capture (R6). The rubric weights unsupported-but-fluent output as a hard fail, not a partial score.
Automation complacency. Reviewers rubber-stamp High tiers once volume climbs.Track accept-rate drift over time. A rising accept rate with a flat correction rate is a warning sign, not a success signal.
Signal staleness. Sponsor-change data is the weakest input and the highest-leverage one.Insufficient-evidence flag rather than imputation.
Cost per account at full book scale.Tiered model routing — cheap pass over the full book, expensive reasoning pass only on accounts that clear a first-pass threshold.

Cut from v1

A renewal-probability percentage, agent-drafted customer emails, and per-segment signal weighting (R9). The percentage was cut on judgment rather than effort: it tested well in concept and would have made the feature demo better, and it would have moved every internal conversation onto the accuracy of a number nobody could defend. Weighting was cut because we did not yet have enough labelled reviewer decisions to tune it honestly — that is now what R6 is accumulating.

09Executive narrative

The one-paragraph version that went upward, which is a different document than everything above:

"Our CSM organization spends more than 200 hours a week on manual account review, and because it is expensive, it runs too late to change renewal outcomes. Two competitors are winning evaluations on risk intelligence we do not have. We are shipping an agent that scores the full book continuously, shows its evidence, and routes every high-risk call to a human before it moves. We expect a 30% reduction in review load. We are not automating the customer relationship — we are giving it back the hours."

Three things that paragraph does deliberately: it opens with the operational cost rather than the technology, it names the competitive stake, and it states the boundary of the automation before anyone asks. Executive alignment on an AI feature is usually lost on the third point, not the first.

10Open questions

  • At what labelled-decision volume does per-segment weighting (R9) become tunable rather than guessed?
  • Does agreement rate stay a real quality signal once CSMs trust the agent, or does it decay into a compliance metric?
  • Where does the abstain threshold sit? Too eager to abstain and the agent stops being useful; too reluctant and it guesses. This is a business decision, not a model parameter.
