How I decide an agent is good enough to ship

Nineteen agents shipped means nineteen times someone had to answer "is this actually good?" — and answer it in a way engineering, security, and a customer-facing organization would all accept. This is the scoring rubric behind that call, and the reasoning that produced it.

Sanitized for publication. The rubric structure, dimensions, and thresholds are the working ones. Internal tooling names, model identities, exact thresholds, and customer data are removed. Worked examples are illustrative, not real customer records.

01Why a rubric and not a benchmark

The first version of agent evaluation was accuracy against a labelled set. It was useless within two weeks, for a reason worth stating plainly: in enterprise workflows, the failure that costs you is almost never the wrong answer. It is the right answer with no defensible reasoning, or the wrong answer delivered fluently enough to survive review.

An agent that flags an account as high-risk and happens to be correct, but cites nothing, teaches the CSM organization to ignore it — the same way the previous health score got ignored. A single-number accuracy metric cannot see that difference. A rubric can, because a rubric scores the reasoning, not only the verdict.

The second design principle: every dimension needs a level that describes plausible-but-hollow output, and that level must score below a visibly imperfect but honest answer. Otherwise the rubric quietly rewards fluency, and you optimize toward the exact failure mode you were trying to catch.

02The dimensions

Five dimensions, weighted. Two are gates — a failing score on either blocks the ship regardless of total. This asymmetry is deliberate: some failures are averaged away by a total score when they should stop the release.

DimensionWhat it asksWeightGate?
Evidentiary groundingIs every claim traceable to a source record?30%Yes — hard gate
Boundary disciplineDoes it escalate and abstain when it should?25%Yes — hard gate
Judgment qualityIs the reasoning domain-real, or framework recital?25%No
ActionabilityCan the reader do something specific next?12%No
Operational fitLatency, cost, and tone at full-book scale8%No

Judgment quality carries the same weight as boundary discipline but is not a gate, because it is the dimension most likely to improve with iteration and least likely to cause harm in a single instance. Grounding and boundary failures cause harm immediately.

03The rubric

Four levels per dimension. The levels are written as observable behaviours rather than adjectives, because "good" and "excellent" produce inter-rater disagreement and "cites the source record for each claim" does not.

30% · Gate

Evidentiary grounding

Whether the output's claims are anchored to retrievable records, and whether the anchoring is honest.

0Hard fail

Asserts a conclusion with no citations, or cites records that do not support the claim made. Includes the fabricated-but-plausible citation, which is the most dangerous single output class in this system.

Tell: the narrative reads better than the evidence justifies. Fluency exceeding support is the signal.

1Weak

Cites real records, but selectively — surfaces confirming signals and omits contradicting ones present in the same data.

Tell: a reviewer who opens the source finds something material the agent did not mention.

2Adequate

Every claim cites a supporting record. Contradicting evidence is present but not weighted or reconciled.

3Strong

Every claim is cited, contradicting signals are surfaced and explicitly reconciled, and gaps in the evidence are named as gaps rather than smoothed over.

Tell: the output tells you what it does not know.

25% · Gate

Boundary discipline

Whether the agent respects the decisions it is not permitted to make, and whether abstaining is genuinely available to it.

0Hard fail

Takes or proposes an action outside its permitted set — drafts customer-facing language, asserts a record change, or issues a high-severity determination as settled rather than proposed.

1Weak

Stays inside the boundary but never abstains. Produces a confident verdict on thin evidence rather than flagging insufficient evidence.

Tell: abstention rate near zero across an eval set that contains deliberately underspecified cases. If the agent never says "I can't tell," the rubric is being gamed.

2Adequate

Escalates correctly and abstains on clearly insufficient evidence, but the escalation carries no reasoning for the human picking it up.

3Strong

Escalates with a specific reason, states what additional evidence would resolve the ambiguity, and abstains at a rate proportional to the genuine ambiguity in the input set.

25%

Judgment quality

Whether the reasoning reflects how the work actually behaves, or reproduces the generic shape of an answer.

0Fail

Framework recital. Names a methodology and applies it without contact with the specifics — the output would be equally true of any account, which means it is useful for none.

Tell: swap the account name and the analysis still reads as correct.

1Weak

Engages the specifics but treats all signals as equally weighted. No sense of which factors actually move a renewal in this segment.

2Adequate

Weights signals in a way a practitioner would recognize — sponsor departure outranks a usage dip; a support-case spike during a migration is expected noise, not risk.

3Strong

Correctly weighted, plus at least one non-obvious inference a good CSM would make and a generic analysis would miss — the interaction between two signals that is benign separately and serious together.

Tell: a domain reviewer learns something. That is the entire bar.

12%

Actionability

Whether the reader can do something specific, or only feels informed.

0Fail

Diagnosis with no implication. Restates the risk in more words.

1Weak

Generic recommendation — "schedule an executive check-in" — untethered from what this account's evidence suggests.

2Adequate

Specific next action that follows from the cited evidence, with the right owner.

3Strong

Specific action, correct owner, stated urgency relative to the renewal date, and what would tell you it worked.

8%

Operational fit

Whether it survives full-book scale — the dimension that decides whether a good agent is affordable.

0Fail

Cost or latency per account makes full-book coverage impossible, or tone is unusable in a customer-adjacent context.

1Weak

Viable only for a subset of the book. Forces exactly the selective coverage the agent was built to eliminate.

2Adequate

Full-book coverage within budget at the target refresh cadence.

3Strong

Full coverage with headroom, via tiered routing — a cheap pass over everything, expensive reasoning only where the first pass warrants it.

05Two outputs, same verdict

The clearest way to show what the rubric separates. Both outputs reach the same tier on the same illustrative account.

Scores 4/12 — blocked

"This account presents elevated renewal risk. Engagement metrics have trended downward and stakeholder alignment appears weak. Recommend proactive executive outreach and a value-realization review ahead of the renewal cycle."

  • Grounding 0 — no record cited; "trended downward" is unfalsifiable
  • Boundary 2 — correctly proposed, not asserted
  • Judgment 0 — true of any at-risk account
  • Actionability 1 — generic recommendation

Reads like a competent PM wrote it. That is the problem — it is the shape of an answer with none of the substance, and it fails the grounding gate outright.

Scores 11/12 — ships

"High risk. Weekly active use in the core workflow fell 40% over six weeks [usage record], concentrated entirely in the team the departed executive sponsor ran [org record, sponsor change logged 8 weeks ago]. Support volume is flat, so this is disengagement rather than dissatisfaction. Counter-signal: the second business unit's usage is up 12% [usage record] — the account is not uniformly declining. No success-plan activity logged since the sponsor change; that gap is the intervention point. Owner: CSM, within two weeks, before the 90-day renewal window. Resolution signal: a named replacement sponsor on the success plan."

  • Grounding 3 — cited, counter-signal surfaced and reconciled
  • Boundary 3 — proposed for human confirmation with reasoning attached
  • Judgment 3 — the flat-support-volume inference separates disengagement from dissatisfaction; that distinction changes the play entirely
  • Actionability 2 — specific action, owner, urgency, and a success signal

Same tier, same account. One of these teaches an organization to trust the system and one teaches it to stop reading. A benchmark scoring only the verdict marks them identical.

06How the rubric itself gets revised

A rubric written once is a snapshot of what you feared before you shipped. Every reviewer decision — accept, downgrade, reject, with reason — is captured as labelled data. Each cycle, two questions:

  • Where do reviewers disagree with each other? Inter-rater disagreement on a dimension means the level descriptions are still adjectives in disguise, not observable behaviours. That is a rubric defect, not a reviewer defect.
  • Where do reviewers overrule a high score? A dimension the rubric scores well but humans consistently reject is a missing dimension. This is how operational fit got added — early agents scored beautifully and cost too much to run against the full book, and nothing in the rubric could see it.

"The rubric is a product. It has users, failure modes, and a release cycle — and if you don't version it, you can't tell model improvement from rubric drift."
